# Frameworks

Load when locking a persona, writing hypotheses, judging whether a problem is worth solving, or
reasoning about price.

---

## Customer slicing — narrow to a who-where pair

> Good segments are a **who-where pair**. If you don't know where to find them, keep slicing.

A vague segment ("students", "parents", "sales orgs") gives you twenty conversations with twenty
different people and contradictory feedback. If you are not hearing consistent problems, the
segment is not specific enough yet.

Slice with these, repeating on any slice that is still generic:

1. Within this group, who would want it most?
2. Would everyone in the group buy it, or only some?
3. Why do they want it — what problem or goal?
4. Does everyone share that motivation, or only some?
5. What other motivations exist?
6. Which other types of people share those motivations?

Then attach behaviour and location — this is what makes a segment findable:

7. What are they already doing to cope (workaround behaviours)?
8. Where can you find the demographic group?
9. Where can you find people doing the workaround?
10. Any group you cannot find? Slice finer until you can.

Pick the slice that scores best on **profitable · easy to reach · rewarding to serve**.

**Missed stakeholders** are their own segments with their own questions: marketplaces (both
sides), kids *and* parents, B2B buying committees (user ≠ budget owner ≠ approver), and critical
partners in manufacturing, distribution or promotion.

## The ideal customer

Built to be blown away by your product. Define by: company type and role · **their problems in
their own words**, the way they would complain to a friend over lunch · budget already allocated
and actively being spent · which of your strengths they specifically need and which of your
weaknesses they do not care about · what would make them stay a year later.

Exclude irrelevant backstory. Only what they are trying to do, why now, how they decide, and
what they can spend.

**Why specificity expands reach:** the perfectly-targeted customer sits at the centre; a ring
roughly 10× larger generally values what you do and shrugs at your gaps; a ring roughly 100×
larger makes an ordinary pros-and-cons trade and still picks you *because* the positioning was
unambiguous. Selling to everyone sells to no one.

**Cancellation rule:** a centre-of-the-bullseye customer who cancels is critical signal; an
outer-ring customer who cancels is noise. Do not steer the product by the outer ring.

## Is there really a problem? Six conditions

Solving a problem is not enough. All six must hold — they multiply.

1. **Plausible** — the market is big enough (roughly 10M+ consumers, or 100k+ companies).
2. **Self-aware** — they know they have the problem and care (vs. ignorance, or "not top three").
3. **Lucrative** — budget is already allocated to *this* problem. Consumers mostly will not pay;
   businesses spend to make or save money.
4. **Liquid** — they can buy *now*: no multi-year lock-in, sunk integration cost, or compliance
   gauntlet in the way.
5. **Eager** — they prefer *you*: they trust you will survive, and you differ on something enough
   of them care about.
6. **Enduring** — they stay, renew, refer a year later. Churn is exponential and always catches
   linear growth.

The interview categories map onto these directly: implications → self-aware; money already spent
→ lucrative; alternatives and the search test → liquid and eager; commitment asks → all of them.
Niching usually wins: fewer people, but higher self-awareness, budget, and differentiation.

## Iterative-hypothesis customer development

The loop this skill implements.

1. **Define what you must learn** — 8–11 objectives: who they are, their workflow, pains,
   coping mechanisms, price tolerance, purchase triggers, hesitations, where they'd hear about
   you, their vocabulary, their underlying motivation. *"What features should I build?"* is
   explicitly not on the list.
2. **Write hypotheses** — one or more per objective, as predictions you could lose. Writing them
   down before the call is what stops confirmation bias afterwards. Expect roughly **half to be
   wrong**; that is the method working.
3. **Turn each hypothesis into an open question** that tests it without hinting at the answer.
   Bad: *"Do you publish often for SEO?"* Good: *"How often did you publish last month — and what
   led you to that rate?"*
4. **Iterate during analysis.** Record answers against each hypothesis. **Surprise signals
   learning** — when one appears, dig, then rewrite the hypotheses and add new ones before the
   next call.
5. **Stop at saturation.** When the surprises stop, learning has stopped.

Two rules worth stating separately:

- **Price early enough to sting.** Naming a real number triggers the visceral reaction that
  matches a real purchase. One founder found that loudly-claimed security concerns evaporated the
  moment a premium was attached to solving them. Do this *without* pitching your product — price
  the *alternatives* they already use.
- **Segment when answers cluster.** If two kinds of person answer differently, keep separate
  hypothesis sheets. Contradictions are the market's structure, not noise in your method.

## Hypothesis scoreboard (Lean Startup)

Every belief that could kill the venture becomes a row. A hypothesis is only useful if an
unexpected answer would change what you build, price, or target.

- **VALIDATED** — two or more independent households/companies gave concrete past-behaviour
  evidence. One enthusiastic interviewee is never validation.
- **REFUTED** — concrete evidence against, or the predicted behaviour is absent where it should
  have shown up.
- **OPEN** — untested, or tested only with contaminated / conflicted sources.

Rules: never move a hypothesis on the strength of a post-pitch answer · a contradiction between
two segments splits the row into two rows, it does not average · every row carries the **next
test** that would settle it · a board with nothing refuted means you are confirming, not testing.

Pair each row with its **leap-of-faith** framing: value hypothesis (do they want it?) and growth
hypothesis (how does the next user arrive?). Interviews can validate the value hypothesis; they
mostly cannot validate the growth one.

## Willingness to pay

Price is not derived from cost; it is derived from what the customer is willing to pay, and WTP
comes from one of three places:

- **Love** — they back your mission, your design, or how you treat them. Produces referrals and
  price tolerance; compounds.
- **Utility** — you solve a practical problem well: convenience, integration, unique capability,
  quality, risk reduction, switching cost. A fair trade; they leave when it stops being useful.
- **Coercion** — lock-in, held data, bundling, monopoly. Produces adversarial customers who
  actively shop for your replacement.

Create value first, split it second: the durable shape is roughly *deliver $4 of value, charge
$2, spend $1*. Aim for the customer getting ~10× what it costs them.

**In interviews you cannot measure WTP by asking for it.** You measure it by: what they already
pay in this category, what they cancelled and why, what they built or hand-made themselves, and
what they did the last time the price went up. Stated prices — especially after a pitch — are
fiction. An owner who invents `"two hundred a month would be a magic number"` for a tablet they have never
held is telling you he likes you.

## Product risk vs market risk

Interviews de-risk **market** questions: do they want it, will they pay, are there enough of
them. They do **not** de-risk **product** questions: can you build it, can you grow it, will they
keep using it.

For marketplaces, network-effect plays, ad models, games and most hard tech, the dominant risk is
in the product — and *"if you can build it, I'd pay"* is burden-shifting, not validation. When an
idea is product-risk-dominant, say so up front: you will have to start building earlier and with
less certainty, and no volume of interviews substitutes for shipping.

## What product/market fit actually looks like

Three things at once, not one:

1. **Pull, not push** — growth rate steps up and stays up, often for reasons you cannot name.
2. **Retention** — cancellation under ~3%/month B2B or ~5%/month B2C. Above that, churn caps
   growth no matter how good acquisition gets.
3. **Critical mass** — enough absolute volume that the first two are not noise.

The felt experience is the shift from *every sale is a struggle* to *we cannot keep up*. The
common shape is linear growth for a couple of years, then a bend, then faster linear growth — not
an exponential curve.

## Extreme questions (use when the bank feels tame)

Push one dimension of reality to an extreme and see what survives: charge 10× · start over with
no customers · offer no support · ship the whole thing in two weeks · charge in a completely
different way · grow with no website · never speak to a customer again, only measure · what is
the mortal wound that kills this company · what is the single thing to do this year if you could
only do one. A design that delivers 60% of the value under an absurd constraint often delivers
2–10× in reality.

## Finding people to interview

Target the real persona only — friends and family cannot validate. Frame as advice-seeking, not
selling. Remove friction (a booking link). Expect ~10% response and do not take it personally.
**End every interview with a referral ask** — one referral each is an infinite supply.

Channels: referrals · role-and-industry search on professional networks, personalised, offering a
fair fee · events your persona attends · online communities where you give first · complaint
monitoring on review sites and social (people *taking action* — switching, building workarounds —
are the best targets: free to complain, expensive to act) · job postings, which spell out pain in
the requirements · paid research panels and expert networks for hard-to-reach roles.

The outreach message has five beats — **Vision · Framing · Weakness · Pedestal · Ask**: half a
sentence on the world you are trying to improve; where you are and what you are looking for; the
specific gap you need help with; why *they* can fill it; then a concrete twenty-minute ask. Do
not open with "can I interview you?", "I'd love your opinion on what we're building", or "got
time for a coffee?" — all three read as a pitch.

**First marketing channel** sits at the intersection of where your customers already are and what
you would genuinely enjoy doing. Being the third-biggest voice on a niche platform beats being
two-hundredth on a giant one. Pick one and go deep.
